# Critical Analysis of a Medical Devices Management Policy

A lifecycle-based critical analysis of a published NHS Trust medical devices
management policy, assessing how well its governance provisions protect patients
and staff across the full medical equipment lifecycle, and specifying the
measurable controls needed to close each gap.

**Domain:** clinical engineering, medical device governance, healthcare quality and risk
**Framework:** seven-phase medical equipment lifecycle (Temple-Bird et al., 2005; WHO, 2011)
**Standards referenced:** ISO 13485, ISO 14971, MHRA Managing Medical Devices, UK Medical Devices Regulations

**Competencies demonstrated:** medical device lifecycle management, regulatory and standards analysis, ISO 14971 risk assessment, governance and KPI design, evidence-based critical analysis

---

## Overview

Medical devices must be governed at every stage of their working life, from the
identification of a clinical need through procurement, acceptance, training,
maintenance, quality assurance, risk management, and eventual disposal. A
weakness in any one phase compounds into patient harm, staff injury, or legal
liability.

This analysis takes a real, published policy, the Management of Medical Devices
Policy of a community NHS Trust serving roughly 455,000 people across 130+
dispersed rural sites with a single external engineering provider, and evaluates
it against the seven-phase medical equipment lifecycle. The Trust's geography
makes it a demanding test: it exposes every weakness a device policy can have,
because there is no in-house engineering fallback and no backup equipment at
remote sites.

The analysis addresses two questions: whether the governance roles and
responsibilities are adequately defined, and whether the policy can actually
achieve its aims across the device lifecycle. Throughout, it does not simply
describe the policy; it identifies each governance gap and specifies the
measurable KPI or control required to close it.

## Approach

The policy is assessed in two parts:

1. **Governance structure.** Each role, from Chief Executive through to
   individual device users and the specialist support functions (Medical Device
   Safety Officer, Medical Engineering Services, Digital Services, Clinical
   Safety Officer), is mapped for its accountability direction, responsibilities,
   governance gap, and the KPI needed to close that gap.
2. **Lifecycle assessment.** The policy is evaluated phase by phase against the
   seven-phase medical equipment lifecycle, with each phase rated and its
   critical gaps identified.

A proactive ISO 14971 risk analysis then scores the key policy gaps by
likelihood and severity, assigning each a residual risk level and a recommended
control.

## Key findings

The policy is well-founded, with a logically layered accountability structure,
thorough acceptance-testing provisions, and a timely Version 6 addition of
connected-device and cybersecurity governance. Applied consistently, it is a
sound foundation.

However, the analysis identifies governance gaps whose consequences fall hardest
on the Trust's most vulnerable points. The most urgent are:

- **No cybersecurity incident response plan** for connected devices. A
  ransomware attack disabling networked infusion pumps across remote clinics
  (the scenario WannaCry demonstrated nationally) would leave teams without
  equipment or a written response procedure.
- **No maintenance prioritisation methodology.** Without criticality scoring, a
  defibrillator at an isolated clinic waits in the same queue as a low-risk
  device at a busy site.
- **No central compliance monitoring** across 130 sites. Accountability is
  placed at the point of care but with no mechanism to verify it is being
  discharged, which the analysis frames as "delegation without oversight."
- **No SLA performance standards** for the single external engineering provider.
  Any deterioration in service is invisible to governance until a patient is
  already at risk.

Each gap is paired with a specific, measurable control. Examples include an
exercised cybersecurity response plan with a four-hour or shorter recovery-time
objective for life-critical devices, and a monthly maintenance dashboard
reporting PPM completion by device criticality tier, maintained at 95% or above.
